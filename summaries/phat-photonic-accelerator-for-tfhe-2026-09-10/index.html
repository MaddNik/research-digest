PHAT: PHotonic Accelerator for TFHE

arXiv · September 2026 · full view ↗

Overview

This paper (arXiv:2609.11613, submitted 10 Sep 2026, authors Guowei Yang, Farbin Fayza, Beren Aydoğan, Carlos A. Ríos Ocampo, Ayse K. Coskun, and Ajay Joshi from Boston University and the University of Maryland, College Park) proposes PHAT, a photonic accelerator for TFHE (Fully Homomorphic Encryption over the Torus). PHAT is built around Optically-addressed Phase-Change Memory (OPCM), a processing-in-memory (PiM) technology, and targets the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) operations that dominate the cost of TFHE's bootstrapping step. The authors report that PHAT is the first TFHE accelerator to use OPCM technology. Compared against the state-of-the-art ASIC accelerator (Morphling), PHAT achieves 1.41×–1.80× higher throughput across four standard bootstrapping parameter sets, and 2.14×–5.10× speedup on four real-world TFHE-based machine-learning (ML) inference workloads.

The core contributions are: (1) a photonic TFHE accelerator architecture based on OPCM; (2) a novel OPCM-based FFT-butterfly-unit (BFU) microarchitecture with a multi-word photonic multiplier that overcomes the limited native precision of an OPCM cell; and (3) a twiddle-stationary dataflow, paired with access-aware BFU allocation and an eager FFT scheduler, that addresses the severe hardware-utilization imbalance created by treating each OPCM device as a fixed, non-volatile twiddle factor.

Motivation: why TFHE needs a new kind of accelerator

FHE allows computation directly on encrypted data, avoiding the need to decrypt sensitive data (e.g., medical or financial records) before processing it on a third-party cloud server. Alternatives such as secure multi-party computation (MPC) incur heavy communication overhead, and trusted execution environments (TEEs) rely on hardware trust assumptions that are vulnerable to side-channel attacks, motivating a non-interactive cryptographic approach such as FHE.

Among FHE schemes (BGV, BFV, CKKS, TFHE), TFHE is distinguished by supporting not just linear arithmetic but also Boolean logic and lookup-table (LUT) based operations with unlimited computation depth, which makes it attractive for general-purpose and ML workloads. Its cost, however, is severe: the paper notes that a 32-bit integer multiplication that takes a few nanoseconds on a 2 GHz CPU can take over 200 ms under TFHE on a CPU. Prior acceleration work spans CPU, GPU, FPGA, and ASIC implementations, and more recently processing-in-memory (PiM) designs on SRAM, DRAM, and ReRAM. All of these remain constrained by the fundamental computational and data-movement overhead of TFHE's large ciphertexts, and digital PiM throughput for TFHE workloads still lags cleartext computation by orders of magnitude.

The dominant cost inside TFHE is bootstrapping, the noise-reset operation needed to support arbitrary-depth computation. The paper reports that bootstrapping consumes about 99% of the runtime of a TFHE gate on a CPU, and that FFT/IFFT operations inside bootstrapping consume about 90% of that bootstrapping time on a modern CPU (Intel Xeon Gold 6426Y, measured by the authors). The paper therefore focuses specifically on accelerating the FFT step of TFHE bootstrapping.

Phase-Change Memory (PCM) is attractive for PiM because of low power and high per-cell storage density (up to 6 bits/cell), and can be optically addressed (OPCM) for even higher compute/communication throughput than electrically-addressed PCM (up to 162 TOPS/mm2 reported for OPCM PiM systems). However, mapping FFT directly onto OPCM is non-trivial: (a) FFT in TFHE typically needs double-precision floating point, far beyond a single OPCM cell's native few bits of precision, and (b) programming (writing) an OPCM cell costs on the order of nanojoules and hundreds of nanoseconds, which would be prohibitive if twiddle factors needed to be reprogrammed frequently. PHAT's architecture, microarchitecture, and dataflow are designed specifically to resolve these two problems.

Background: OPCM devices

An OPCM (also called a GST, for Ge2Sb2Te5) cell is an optical waveguide with a cladding-embedded GST patch that can be reversibly switched between amorphous and crystalline phases using heat. Because partial crystallization states are achievable, the cell supports semi-continuous (multi-bit) modulation of optical transmittance: crystalline GST absorbs much more light than amorphous GST, so the phase state sets the waveguide's transmittance level.

Writing (programming) a cell changes its phase via heat, delivered either electrically (through embedded microheaters, inducing Joule heating) or optically (via self-heating from absorbed pump pulses). Reported switching energies are as low as 5.55 nJ (amorphization) and 860.71 nJ (crystallization) for electrical microheater switching, versus only 460 pJ and 140 pJ respectively for optical switching. However, optical switching does not scale well: routing individually addressed optical pump pulses requires complex, power-hungry switching networks, or dedicated per-cell coupling gratings that roughly 6× the device footprint (from about $20\times20\ \mu m^2$ to $50\times50\ \mu m^2$). The paper therefore adopts electrical switching for programming, despite its higher energy cost, because of its scalability and CMOS compatibility.

Up to 64 deterministic states (6 bits) have been demonstrated in a single GST cell. The multilevel property also enables analog in-memory computation: a scalar-scalar multiplication $p = u \cdot v$ can be performed by encoding the multiplicand $u$ into the cell's optical transmittance and modulating the multiplier $v$ onto the amplitude of an input light pulse; the attenuated output intensity is the product $u \cdot v$. Arranging GST cells into crossbar arrays realizes efficient matrix-vector multiplication (MVM), useful for ML inference. PCM is preferred over other non-volatile PiM materials (ferroelectric, magneto-optic, memristive) because it supports non-volatile amplitude modulation without needing external electric/magnetic fields for readout, has long-term (decade-scale) retention, and offers the largest demonstrated refractive-index modulation among known materials, all with a compact footprint (less than $10\ \mu m^2$) compared to Mach-Zehnder Interferometer (MZI) or micro-ring resonator (MRR) based phase modulators.

Background: TFHE data structures and operations

TFHE relies on two core homomorphic operations: homomorphic addition, and Programmable BootStrapping (PBS). Unlike bootstrapping in other FHE schemes (which only resets noise), PBS also evaluates an arbitrary univariate function on the encrypted data, effectively evaluating an encrypted lookup table. Together, homomorphic addition and PBS let TFHE evaluate arbitrary Boolean, arithmetic, and relational functions.

TFHE uses four main data structures, with typical parameter ranges shown below:

ParameterDescriptionTypical value
$n$Dimension of LWE ciphertext$2^8$–$2^{12}$
$N$Polynomial degree$2^{10}$–$2^{14}$
$k$Dimension of GLWE ciphertext1–4
$l_b$Decomposition level of bootstrapping2–4
  • LWE ciphertext: a vector of $(n+1)$ scalar elements $[a_1,\dots,a_n,b]$, each a 32-bit or 64-bit integer; this is the main encryption format.
  • GLWE ciphertext: a vector of $(k+1)$ degree-$N$ polynomials $[A_1(X),\dots,A_k(X),B(X)]$, used to store the univariate functions used during PBS.
  • Bootstrapping Key (BSK): a vector of $n$ GGSW (General Gentry-Sahai-Waters) ciphertexts, each a $(k+1)\cdot l_b \times (k+1)$ matrix of degree-$N$ polynomials.
  • Key Switching Key (KSK): a vector of $k\cdot N\cdot l_k$ individual LWE ciphertexts.

TFHE's polynomial degree $N$ is much smaller than in schemes like CKKS (which can use $N=2^{16}$), which matters here because OPCM area scales with $N$; a smaller $N$ keeps the OPCM footprint compact.

Programmable bootstrapping has three steps: (1) modulus switching, moving each LWE element from its original modulus $q$ to the new modulus $2N$ (cheap, since $N$ is a power of two); (2) blind rotation, which is by far the most expensive step, consuming about 96% of total PBS runtime, and consists of repeated polynomial rotation/subtraction, decomposition, and external-product (polynomial multiplication) operations; and (3) sample extraction, which involves no computation, only memory access and data regrouping, selecting a specific coefficient from the resulting GLWE ciphertext to form the new LWE ciphertext. The full algorithm is given in the Algorithms section at the end of this summary (Algorithm 1).

Key switching (KS) converts an LWE ciphertext (produced under a different key after PBS, sized $k\cdot(N+1)$ scalars) back to the original key's ciphertext of $(n+1)$ scalars. It decomposes the input, expanding the first $k\cdot N$ elements into a vector of length $k\cdot N\cdot l_k$ (where $l_k$ is the KS decomposition level), and multiplies this by a precomputed matrix of dimensions $(k\cdot N\cdot l_k)\times(n+1)$.

Linear leveled operations (ciphertext-ciphertext addition, ciphertext-cleartext addition, ciphertext-cleartext multiplication) reduce to simple element-wise polynomial additions or small-constant polynomial multiplications across the $(k+1)$ polynomials of a GLWE ciphertext.

Background: FFT and its role in TFHE

FFT and inverse FFT (IFFT) accelerate the polynomial multiplications that are the bottleneck of PBS. A Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) converts a length-$N$ sequence from time domain to frequency domain:

$$Y[k] = \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} X[i]\,\omega_N^{ik}, \qquad 0 \le k < N,\qquad \omega_N = \exp(-2\pi j k / N),\ j=\sqrt{-1}$$

The powers of $\omega_N$ are called twiddle factors. Direct DFT computation costs $O(N^2)$; the Cooley-Tukey radix-2 FFT algorithm reduces this to $O(N\log N)$ by dividing the size-$N$ transform into $\log N$ stages, each containing $N/2$ butterfly operations. A single butterfly operation takes two complex inputs $(a,b)$ and one complex twiddle factor $\omega$, producing two complex outputs:

$$(x,y) = (a + \omega b,\ a - \omega b) \tag{1}$$

Expanding into real and imaginary parts:

$$\mathrm{Re}(x) = \mathrm{Re}(a) + [\mathrm{Re}(\omega)\mathrm{Re}(b) - \mathrm{Im}(\omega)\mathrm{Im}(b)]$$ $$\mathrm{Re}(y) = \mathrm{Re}(a) - [\mathrm{Re}(\omega)\mathrm{Re}(b) - \mathrm{Im}(\omega)\mathrm{Im}(b)]$$ $$\mathrm{Im}(x) = \mathrm{Im}(a) + [\mathrm{Re}(\omega)\mathrm{Im}(b) + \mathrm{Im}(\omega)\mathrm{Re}(b)]$$ $$\mathrm{Im}(y) = \mathrm{Im}(a) - [\mathrm{Re}(\omega)\mathrm{Im}(b) + \mathrm{Im}(\omega)\mathrm{Re}(b)] \tag{2}$$

Multiplying two degree-$N$ polynomials $A$ and $B$ naively costs $O(N^2)$, but this can be reduced to $O(N \log N)$ via

$$A \cdot B = \mathrm{IFFT}(\mathrm{FFT}(A) * \mathrm{FFT}(B)) \tag{3}$$

where $*$ denotes element-wise multiplication in the frequency domain. TFHE actually needs a negacyclic FFT of size $2N$ for degree-$N$ polynomial multiplication. This can be computed with a size-$N$ ordinary FFT by first pre-multiplying the polynomial's coefficients by powers of the $2N$-th root of unity $\omega_{2N}$:

$$\hat A = \left(A[0],\ \omega_{2N} A[1],\ \dots,\ \omega_{2N}^{N-1} A[N-1]\right)$$

Because the polynomial coefficients are real (imaginary parts are zero), conjugate symmetry lets the FFT size be halved again, from $N$ to $N/2$: pack $A[i]$ and $A[i+N/2]$ into one complex value $A[i] + jA[i+N/2]$ and run a size-$N/2$ FFT. This two-stage size reduction (negacyclic preprocessing, then conjugate-symmetry folding) is the standard trick the paper adopts from prior TFHE implementations, and it sets the actual FFT sizes that PHAT's hardware must support.

PHAT system architecture

PHAT consists of a host processor and a 2.5D-integrated accelerator built from multiple chiplets on an interposer: an electrical chiplet, a DRAM chiplet (HBM2), one or more laser source chiplets, and multiple OPCM chiplets, each containing many photonic Butterfly Units (BFUs). The electrical chiplet connects to all OPCM and DRAM chiplets in a star topology and acts as the central control/communication hub; the host processor and main memory talk to the accelerator over the system bus.

The electrical chiplet integrates control logic, SRAM buffers, a memory interface, Vector Processing Units (VPUs), and General Matrix Multiply (GEMM) units (implemented as systolic arrays). BFUs (in the OPCM chiplets) execute the FFT butterfly operations, the primary computational bottleneck; VPUs handle element-wise vector operations, and GEMM units handle matrix multiplications, covering the remaining TFHE operations.

Each OPCM chiplet holds an array of BFUs, plus electronic peripheral circuits, programming circuitry, and control logic. Each BFU is dedicated to one specific twiddle factor, and every twiddle factor is assigned to one or more BFUs. Because OPCM is non-volatile, once the arrays inside a BFU are programmed with a twiddle factor for a given set of TFHE parameters, they never need to be refreshed for that parameter set.

Execution flow: the host copies data from main memory into the accelerator's DRAM (analogous to a CPU-GPU memcopy); the controller then programs all OPCM arrays inside the BFUs during initialization; the host then invokes the accelerator for TFHE operations (encryption/decryption, ciphertext addition, PBS, etc.); the controller manages data transfer between DRAM and SRAM buffers and schedules VPU, GEMM, and FFT operations; the host can DMA additional data in/out while the accelerator runs, hiding transfer latency; finally, results are copied back to main memory.

BFU and OPCM photonic multiplier (PMult) microarchitecture

Each BFU implements the Cooley-Tukey radix-2 butterfly (Equation 1 above) using its assigned constant twiddle factor $\omega$. Expanding into real/imaginary parts (Equation 2), each butterfly requires exactly four unique multiplications (real-times-real and real-times-imaginary combinations of $\omega$ and $b$, where $b$ is the variable input and $\omega$ is the fixed constant), plus additions/subtractions. The four multiplications are performed optically using OPCM's high-throughput analog computation and PiM capability; the additions/subtractions are done with conventional electronic adder logic. A BFU therefore contains four OPCM Photonic Multiplier (PMult) units, an Add/Subtract (AS) unit, control logic, and the programming circuitry for its OPCM array. Each signed PMult is built from two unsigned PMult units.

Numerical accuracy model. The design's correctness analysis is split into a digital domain (all digital components, including shift-and-add logic following optical-to-electrical conversion) and an analog domain (OPCM and electrical-optical-electrical, E-O-E, conversions). In the digital domain, the authors experimentally determine the bit-width needed for correct PBS by plugging their accuracy model into the TFHE-rs software library. In the analog domain, the system is engineered so that analog computation error stays below the ADC quantization threshold (effectively zero error rate contributed by analog components), by choosing laser power based on required output SNR while accounting for optical losses and photodetector sensitivity.

Precision requirement. A single OPCM cell can store at most a 6-bit multiplicand and perform a 6-bit by 6-bit multiplication by light attenuation. Software TFHE libraries typically use double-precision floating point (53-bit mantissa) for FFT, far beyond a single cell's capability. Sweeping FFT bit-precision under TFHE parameter set IV (see the parameter table below), the authors find that PBS success rate only reaches 100% once FFT precision reaches at least 42 bits.

Multi-word multiplication. To reach that precision using low-precision OPCM cells, PHAT decomposes each operand into $B$-bit words. For a 3-word example, operands $u$ and $v$ are split into $u_2,u_1,u_0$ and $v_2,v_1,v_0$ (most to least significant, each $b$ bits):

$$u = 2^{2b}u_2 + 2^b u_1 + u_0, \qquad v = 2^{2b}v_2 + 2^b v_1 + v_0 \tag{4}$$

The product is computed by the textbook multi-word method, taking all pairwise products $u_i v_j$ (9 partial products for the 3-word example, each $2b$ bits) and summing by weight:

$$p = \underbrace{(u_2 v_2)}_{p_4}\cdot 2^{4b} + \underbrace{(u_2 v_1 + u_1 v_2)}_{p_3}\cdot 2^{3b} + \underbrace{(u_2 v_0 + u_1 v_1 + u_0 v_2)}_{p_2}\cdot 2^{2b} + \underbrace{(u_1 v_0 + u_0 v_1)}_{p_1}\cdot 2^{b} + \underbrace{u_0 v_0}_{p_0} \tag{5}$$

Note each $p_i$ can exceed $2b$ bits due to carries from the sum.

OPCM PMult architecture. A regular OPCM array is a waveguide crossbar where each GST cell's transmittance encodes a matrix element and laser amplitude encodes an input vector element; electrical-optical (E-O) converters modulate laser amplitudes per row, Directional Couplers (DC) broadcast each row's laser across the row's GST cells, GST cells multiply by attenuation, and column waveguides sum the attenuated (partial-product) signals which are then read out by optical-electrical (O-E) converters, computing a full matrix-vector multiplication (MVM) in one pass.

The 9-term sum in Equation 5 can be organized as two smaller triangular MVMs: a $1\times3$ by $3\times3$ MVM producing $(p_2,p_3,p_4)$, and a $1\times2$ by $2\times2$ MVM producing $(p_0,p_1)$. Rather than wasting area on two separate arrays with many zero entries, PHAT combines both triangular matrices into a single square OPCM array by breaking the 2nd and 3rd column waveguides and inserting extra O-E converters, disconnecting the upper and lower triangles so each can be read independently while sharing the same physical cells and rows. This saves nearly half the OPCM area versus a naive two-array mapping and also reduces the number of E-O conversions needed since input lasers are shared per row.

To compute a high-precision multiplication, the OPCM array is first programmed with all the $u_i$ words (Equation 5, with $u$ held as a constant). The lower triangle computes $(p_2,p_3,p_4)$ and the upper triangle computes $(p_0,p_1)$; O-E conversion brings the partial products into the digital domain, where Shift-and-Add (S+A) logic combines them into the final product. If a partial product is wider than the ADC can represent, only the most significant bits are retained, introducing a small, bounded approximation error. Multiple S+A units operate time-multiplexed to keep pace with OPCM's high modulation rate.

Because laser intensity and GST transmittance are both strictly non-negative, an unsigned PMult can only multiply non-negative variables by positive constants. To support a negative constant $u$, it is split into positive and negative parts $u^+=\max(0,u)$ and $u^-=\max(0,-u)$, stored in two separate OPCM arrays, and the signed product is recovered as $v\cdot u = v\times u^+ - v\times u^-$, with the subtraction performed in the analog-electrical domain after O-E conversion. To also support a negative variable input $v$, the design offsets $v$ and adds an extra row of OPCM cells to each triangular subarray, preloaded with precomputed constants equal to the sum of the remaining elements in that column (values fixed by the twiddle-factor words).

Twiddle-stationary dataflow and access-aware BFU allocation

Because OPCM is non-volatile and reprogramming a cell costs 5.55–860.71 nJ and about 400 ns, PHAT programs each BFU with one specific, fixed twiddle factor before an FFT run begins, and keeps it stationary for the lifetime of a set of TFHE parameters (a twiddle-stationary dataflow). Every butterfly operation that needs a given twiddle factor must be routed to the BFU(s) holding that factor. The authors state this is, to their knowledge, the first FFT dataflow designed specifically to minimize twiddle-factor reprogramming cost by keeping factors stationary across many FFT invocations.

Reprogramming is avoided even when the workload's TFHE parameters change, because the twiddle factors needed for a size-$2^n$ FFT are a superset of those needed for any smaller size-$2^m$ FFT ($m

Access imbalance problem. Because each BFU is now bound to one twiddle factor, BFU utilization is entirely data (access-pattern) dependent. In the first FFT stage, for example, every butterfly needs the same twiddle factor, so all of them queue for one BFU, driving utilization as low as 0.8% for a size-1024 FFT. Measured twiddle-factor access counts for a size-1024 FFT are extremely skewed (heavily favoring a handful of factors).

Access-aware BFU allocation. To fix this, PHAT allocates more BFUs to the most frequently accessed twiddle factors, proportional to access frequency, while guaranteeing at least one BFU per factor. This trades chip area for reduced contention. A configurable allocation threshold controls the trade-off: if a twiddle factor's access count exceeds the threshold, extra BFUs proportional to its access frequency are allocated; factors below the threshold get exactly one BFU. A lower threshold gives more BFUs (less contention, more area); threshold $=N-1$ gives the degenerate case of one BFU per factor (no mitigation).

Sweeping the threshold for a size-1024 FFT: for higher thresholds (threshold $\ge 15$), speedup ranges from $1.21\times$ to $13.38\times$ with modest area overhead of 0.2%–37.5%. For lower thresholds (threshold $\le 7$), speedup can exceed $100\times$ but area overhead grows up to $5.5\times$, judged impractical for large FFT sizes. Fully removing the imbalance (BFUs strictly proportional to access frequency, effectively threshold $=1$ for all factors) would require 4.5× more area, which the authors deem impractical. Based on this trade-off, PHAT adopts threshold $=15$, giving a $13.38\times$ speedup for only 37.5% area overhead; this configuration is used for the rest of the evaluation.

FFT scheduling: eager scheduling

Running a single FFT under the twiddle-stationary dataflow activates only a limited subset of BFUs at any time, since only BFUs holding the currently needed twiddle factor(s) can be active. Running multiple FFTs concurrently helps because FFTs at different progress stages are likely to need different twiddle factors at the same time, spreading load across more BFUs.

PHAT's eager scheduling dispatches butterfly operations from multiple in-flight FFTs (all belonging to one PBS invocation) as soon as their needed BFU becomes available, i.e. out-of-order execution across FFTs, rather than lock-step pipelining of FFTs stage-by-stage. This improves BFU utilization, and the OPCM FFT units run concurrently with the VPUs and GEMM units on the electrical chiplet.

Measured with varying numbers of FFTs in the schedule queue, eager scheduling alone gives only marginal gains (up to about 20% throughput improvement even with 50 queued FFTs), which shows the dominant bottleneck is access imbalance, not lack of scheduling opportunities. Access-aware BFU allocation alone gives the bulk of the improvement ($13.38\times$). Combining both (ES+AA) gives substantially more than either alone, with speedup growing as more FFTs become available in the queue, reaching over $70\times$ improvement in FFT throughput at the largest queue depths tested.

Hardware implementation. Rather than build complex online scheduling logic, PHAT relies on an offline scheduler: once TFHE parameters are fixed, the size, number, and arrival order of all FFTs within a PBS invocation are known in advance, so the full eager-scheduling execution plan (including all butterfly assignments) is precomputed offline and loaded into PHAT's SRAM buffers. At runtime, PHAT's control logic only needs simple state machines to step through this precomputed plan, avoiding runtime scheduling overhead.

Difference from prior batching. The authors note this differs fundamentally from prior TFHE batching strategies, which use simple lock-step pipelining across batched FFTs. PHAT's eager scheduling instead maximizes the diversity of twiddle factors accessed at any given time by allowing out-of-order progress, which is more complex to implement but essential to achieving good BFU utilization on OPCM hardware where the resource-to-twiddle-factor binding is fixed.

Evaluation methodology

Accuracy is evaluated using two existing software libraries, TFHE-rs and Concrete-ML. The default FFT calls in TFHE-rs are replaced with a custom FFT model reproducing OPCM PMult's quantization/precision behavior, and PBS success rate is measured under this substitution. TFHE-rs is also modified to trace ciphertext-level operations within PBS for detailed power/performance/area (PPA) modeling.

Real-world TFHE application traces (TFHE parameters and operation counts) are extracted by compiling and running applications with Concrete-ML, and these drive an in-house performance simulator that reads operation traces and schedules them onto PHAT's hardware model. A custom simulator models the twiddle-stationary FFT dataflow with access-aware allocation and eager scheduling to get precise cycle counts; GEMM and VPU units are modeled by extending ScaleSim, an open-source systolic-array simulator.

Area model. PHAT is a 2.5D chiplet design: an electrical chiplet, a DRAM chiplet, a laser-source chiplet, and multiple OPCM chiplets, connected by dedicated electrical links on an interposer. To support the largest evaluated PBS configurations and real-world workloads, the system uses 4 OPCM chiplets, each with a $16\times11$ array of BFUs occupying $84\ mm^2$. Each BFU has 4 PMult units, and each PMult uses 2 OPCM arrays of size $7\times9$, supporting 7-word multiplications. Each OPCM cell occupies $30\times30\ \mu m^2$; photodetectors as small as $4\times10\ \mu m^2$ can be integrated within that footprint. Electrical peripherals (programming circuitry, S+A logic) sit on a separate overlapping layer and add no extra footprint. The electrical chiplet contains $32\times48$ GEMM units (each a $4\times4$ systolic array), 16 VPUs, and 81 MB of SRAM, operating at 1 GHz over $181\ mm^2$. The HBM2 DRAM chiplet occupies $92\ mm^2$.

Frequency and power model. OPCM devices have been demonstrated at up to 18 GHz, but the authors conservatively set PHAT's OPCM operating frequency to 5 GHz (a level achievable in CMOS). Total power includes laser power (from optical losses: $0.6\ dB$ GST-cell loss, $0.0028\ dB$ waveguide-crossing loss, $0.01\ dB$ directional-coupler loss, with 10% combined laser+photodetector quantum efficiency) and electrical power. Programming cost is amortized/excluded from PPA since twiddle factors are programmed once per benchmark. E-O conversion costs $1\ pJ/bit$; O-E conversion at 5 GS/s costs $7.4\ mW$. CMOS arithmetic components are synthesized with the ASAP7 node using SiliconCompiler; SRAM PPA comes from a memory compiler (1 GHz, bank-interleaved to match the 5 GHz OPCM rate). HBM2 DRAM access is modeled at $3.9\ pJ/bit$, $40\ ns$ latency, with $64\ GB/s$ system-bus bandwidth. PHAT consumes 528 W on average across the four real-world workloads evaluated.

Results: accuracy of the OPCM FFT unit

A single OPCM cell only supports up to 6-bit resolution, so the multi-word PMult aggregates several cells to reach the precision PBS requires. Precision can be increased either by adding more words or more bits per word. Measured as Mean Squared Error (MSE) against a double-precision floating-point baseline (53-bit mantissa) for a size-1024 FFT, increasing either the word count or bits-per-word reduces error, as expected.

Sweeping PMult configurations against full PBS success rate under parameter set IV (128-bit security), each data point averaged over 100 PBS runs (success meaning correct noise reset and correct cleartext result after decryption), the smallest PMult configuration achieving 100% success is a 7-word unit with 6 bits per word, giving $7\times6+1=43$ bits of total precision (the extra bit is for sign). This configuration is used for all subsequent PHAT evaluation.

Results: effect of access-aware BFU allocation and scheduling

Sweeping the access-aware allocation threshold for a size-1024 FFT confirms the design choice discussed earlier: thresholds $\ge15$ give speedups of $1.21\times$–$13.38\times$ at 0.2%–37.5% area overhead; thresholds $\le7$ push speedup above $100\times$ in some cases but at up to $5.5\times$ area overhead, which is impractical for large FFT sizes. PHAT's chosen operating point (threshold $=15$) achieves $13.38\times$ speedup with 37.5% area overhead.

Sweeping the number of FFTs in the scheduling queue (0 to 50) and comparing Eager Scheduling alone (ES), Access-Aware allocation alone (AA), and both combined (ES+AA) against a plain baseline: ES alone yields only marginal gains (up to about 20% even at 50 queued FFTs), confirming that access imbalance, not scheduling opportunity, is the primary bottleneck. AA alone delivers the bulk of the improvement, $13.38\times$. ES+AA combined scales further as queue depth grows, reaching over $70\times$ throughput improvement, showing the two techniques are complementary: AA balances per-factor contention, and ES exploits the resulting availability across concurrently in-flight FFTs.

Morphling already achieves over $100\times$ speedup versus the CPU baseline, and PHAT further improves on that. Real-world applications show larger PHAT gains than isolated PBS benchmarks because they also involve GEMM and vector operations (e.g., ciphertext addition) that run in parallel with the BFUs for most of the execution time, hiding BFU latency behind GEMM/VPU work and lowering overall execution time.

Power and area. PHAT consumes 8.1× more area and 10× higher power than Morphling. The authors attribute this to the relative immaturity of integrated photonics compared to highly scaled electrical ASIC processes, and offer two mitigating considerations: (1) photonic components are typically fabricated on older, more mature CMOS nodes (130 nm or above), which have higher yield and lower cost per unit area, so the larger area need not translate into prohibitive fabrication cost; and (2) photonic integration is an active research area, and future improvements in modulator/detector efficiency, integration density, low-loss waveguides, and materials are expected to reduce power consumption. The authors frame PHAT as a proof-of-concept, first-of-its-kind exploration of OPCM for PiM-based FHE acceleration rather than an immediately fabrication-optimal design; while not yet area/power-competitive with ASICs, they argue its architectural principles (twiddle-stationary dataflow, access-aware allocation, eager scheduling) lay groundwork for future systems that benefit from continued photonics advances.

Related work

Conventional TFHE accelerators span CPUs, GPUs, FPGAs, and ASICs, applying algorithmic, architectural, and device-level optimizations; none escape TFHE's fundamental computation and data-movement costs. PHAT instead leverages OPCM's PiM and high throughput to attack the same bottleneck from a different substrate.

PiM FHE accelerators using SRAM, DRAM, or ReRAM reduce data movement but remain limited by conventional electronic operating frequencies and technology-scaling limits. PHAT differs by computing in the analog optical domain at much higher operating frequencies, aiming for substantially higher computational and communication throughput.

Photonic ML accelerators (using MRR, MZI, and OPCM devices) have targeted GEMM and vector dot-product operations for ML, typically at low precision (8–12 bits), which suffices for ML's noise-tolerant workloads but is far short of TFHE's $\ge$42-bit requirement. PHAT's contribution here is a photonic multi-word multiplier that reaches the needed precision without resorting to additional mechanisms like RNS (Residue Number System) conversion used in some prior photonic accelerators (e.g., Mirage), making it a more natural fit for TFHE's precision demands.

Conclusion

TFHE enables privacy-preserving cloud computation but is bottlenecked by the FFT operations inside programmable bootstrapping, and existing electronic accelerators are limited by technology scaling and memory-wall effects. PHAT is proposed as an OPCM-based photonic accelerator purpose-built for TFHE, combining a novel electro-photonic BFU/PMult microarchitecture, a twiddle-stationary dataflow, access-aware BFU allocation, and eager FFT scheduling to address OPCM's analog precision limits and programming overhead. The paper reports $1.39\times$–$1.77\times$ (elsewhere stated as $1.41\times$–$1.80\times$) speedup over the SOTA ASIC accelerator for standalone PBS, and $2.14\times$–$5.10\times$ speedup on real-world TFHE ML inference workloads, at the cost of substantially higher area (8.1×) and power (10×) than the ASIC baseline, which the authors attribute to the current immaturity of integrated photonics rather than a fundamental architectural limitation.

Algorithms

The source paper presents one explicit algorithm listing, Algorithm 1 (Programmable Bootstrapping / PBS), reproduced below verbatim in pseudocode form. This is the procedure that PHAT's BFUs, VPUs, and GEMM units collectively execute; the nested loop over decomposition levels and GLWE dimensions is where the FFT/IFFT calls that PHAT accelerates are invoked.

Algorithm 1: Programmable Bootstrapping
Input:
  n, k        : dimension of LWE/GLWE ciphertext
  l_b         : bootstrapping decomposition level
  N           : number of coefficients in polynomial
  c[n+1]      : LWE ciphertext, each element is a scalar
  tv[k+1]     : test vector, each element is an N-degree polynomial
  bsk[n][(k+1)*l_b][k+1] : bootstrapping key, each element is an N-degree polynomial

Output:
  o[k*N+1]    : LWE ciphertext, each element is a scalar

Local variables:
  etv[(k+1)*l_b], otv[k+1]   (initialized to zero)

for ii = 0 to (n+1):
    c[ii] = ModSwitch(c[ii])

tv = RotateLeft(tv, c[n])

for ii = 0 to n:
    tv = tv - RotateRight(tv, c[ii])
    etv = Decompose(tv, l_b)              // Decomposition
    for jj = 0 to (k+1):
        for kk = 0 to (k+1)*l_b:
            otv[jj] = otv[jj] + IFFT( FFT(etv[kk]) * FFT(bsk[ii][kk][jj]) )
    tv = otv
    InitZero(otv)

o = SampleExtract(tv)                     // Sample Extraction
return o

Notes on the algorithm as described in the paper: the outer loop over ii from 0 to n implements the sequential blind rotation stage (the most expensive part of PBS, about 96% of PBS runtime), where each iteration performs a polynomial rotation and subtraction, a decomposition into l_b levels, and an external-product style accumulation realized as pairs of FFT / IFFT calls (the target of PHAT's acceleration) and additions. The final SampleExtract step (sample extraction) requires no arithmetic, only memory access and data regrouping to pull out a single coefficient of the resulting GLWE ciphertext as the new LWE ciphertext. Modulus switching, in the first loop, is computationally cheap since N is always a power of two.

Beyond this explicit algorithm box, the paper also describes, in prose rather than boxed pseudocode, the multi-word multiplication procedure (Equations 4-5, mapped onto the OPCM PMult architecture), the access-aware BFU allocation policy (allocate BFUs to a twiddle factor in proportion to its access count once that count exceeds a configurable threshold, otherwise allocate exactly one BFU), and the eager scheduling policy (dispatch each butterfly operation as soon as its assigned BFU is free, computed offline into a static execution plan). These are procedural/architectural techniques rather than formal algorithms, and are covered in the corresponding sections above rather than restated here as pseudocode, to avoid presenting the paper's design descriptions as if they were algorithm listings it did not present as such.
